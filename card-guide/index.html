BEGINNER-FRIENDLY POKÉMON CARD GUIDE

Value It. Grade It. Keep It—or Sell It.

Pulled something exciting? This guide walks you through the whole process, from protecting the card to figuring out what it is, what it may be worth, whether grading makes sense, and what your selling options look like.

1Protect 2Identify 3Value 4Condition 5Grade 6Keep or Sell
1

FIRST THINGS FIRST

Protect Your Card Immediately

Before worrying about how much a card is worth, protect it. Pokémon cards can be damaged surprisingly easily. Scratches, whitening, bent corners, fingerprints, dents, and creases can all reduce the card’s condition—and potentially its value.

Start with a penny sleeve

Hold the card carefully by its edges and gently slide it into the sleeve. Avoid touching the front or back surface more than necessary.

2

KNOW WHAT YOU PULLED

Identify the Exact Card

Two cards featuring the same Pokémon can look similar but have dramatically different values. Start with the card name, then find the collector number near the bottom of the card.

A useful search combines both. For example: Charizard ex 223/197. That exact card number is a Special Illustration Rare from Obsidian Flames.

It is also completely possible for the first number to be higher than the printed set total. Special-rarity cards can be numbered beyond the main set.

Verify all of these

  • Card name
  • Card number
  • Artwork
  • Set
  • Rarity / version
  • Language
  • Holographic or special treatment
TCGplayerLook up the exact card and compare listings → CollectrSearch cards and track market estimates →

Watch for different versions

Cards can have standard, reverse holographic, illustration rare, special illustration rare, ultra rare, hyper rare, promotional, and other special versions. The exact version matters.

3

CHECK THE MARKET

Find the Current Value

Once you’ve identified the exact card, use pricing resources such as TCGplayer and Collectr as starting points. No single website can guarantee what someone will actually pay.

Compare apples to apples

Make sure you’re comparing the exact card, set, variation, language, and similar condition. Compare raw cards with raw cards and graded cards with the same grading company and grade.

Don’t confuse asking price with market value. Anyone can list a card for any amount. Whenever possible, look at recent completed sales—not just active listings.
4

LOOK CLOSELY

Evaluate the Condition

Use good lighting, handle the card by its edges, and inspect both the front and back.

Centering

Are the borders reasonably even, or is the artwork noticeably shifted toward one side?

Corners

Look for whitening, bending, fraying, dings, or rounded corners.

Edges

Check for white spots, chipping, rough cuts, edge wear, or small dents.

Surface

Tilt the card under bright light and look for scratches, scuffs, print lines, roller marks, indentations, or holo scratches.

Dents & Creases

Even a small indentation that is hard to see straight-on can significantly affect a professional grade.

Back of the Card

Don’t stop after the artwork. Many clean-looking cards have whitening or scratches on the reverse.

5

IS GRADING WORTH IT?

Compare Your Grading Options

Professional grading authenticates and evaluates a card, assigns a grade, and seals it in a protective holder or “slab.” Grading can add value—but not every card should be graded.

PSA

One of the most recognized names in Pokémon grading with a large resale market. PSA can be expensive, and grading results can be subjective or inconsistent with collector expectations. Never assume a clean-looking card will receive a 10.

Visit PSA →

CGC

An established grading option with a strong trading-card presence. Collectors may prefer its grading scale, slab design, turnaround options, or Pristine 10 tier.

Visit CGC →

Beckett / BGS

A long-established grader known for detailed grading and the difficult Black Label 10. The resale premium varies by card and grade.

Visit Beckett →

TAG

A technology-focused option using advanced imaging and machine-learning-assisted analysis, with a detailed digital grading report. It can be especially interesting if you value transparency into identified defects and the grading process.

Visit TAG →

Does grading make financial sense?

Before submitting a card, compare four things:

1Raw card value2Likely grade3Value at that grade4Total grading + shipping cost

Example: Raw value $80 · Grade 10 value $250 · Grade 9 value $95 · Total grading/shipping cost $40

A 10 could create meaningful upside. But if it receives a 9, you may have spent $40 to turn an $80 raw card into something worth around $95.

Would you still be happy you graded it if it gets a 9 instead of a 10?

6

YOUR CARD, YOUR CHOICE

Keep It or Sell It?

Reasons to Keep It

  • It’s your favorite Pokémon.
  • You love the artwork.
  • You pulled it yourself.
  • You’re completing the set.
  • It has sentimental value.
  • You think you’d regret selling it.

A card’s market price isn’t the same as its value to you.

Reasons to Sell It

  • You don’t collect that Pokémon or set.
  • You’d rather buy another card you really want.
  • You want to fund more packs or another hobby.
  • The card represents a meaningful amount of money.
  • You simply prefer cash over owning it.

Where can you sell Pokémon cards?

The percentages below are rough estimates of what you might actually receive compared with a realistic market value. They are not guarantees. Card desirability, condition, fees, shipping, current demand, and negotiation all matter.

eBay

~80–87%

Best for: Sellers willing to do more work for potentially stronger pricing and a large audience.

Marketplace fees, shipping, and optional promotion reduce your net proceeds.

TCGplayer

~82–86%

Best for: Raw cards and access to buyers already shopping specifically for trading cards.

Marketplace, transaction, and shipping costs reduce your net.

Local Card Shop

~50–70%

Best for: Speed and convenience.

The store needs enough margin to cover overhead, resale risk, and profit. Extremely desirable cards may receive stronger offers.

Card Show / Dealer

~65–85%

Best for: Comparing multiple cash offers and negotiating in person.

Dealers still need resale margin, but competition between buyers can help.

Private Collector Sale

~90–100%

Best for: Potentially maximizing net proceeds when you know how to transact safely.

Be cautious about fake payments, chargebacks, shipping disputes, impersonation scams, and unfamiliar buyers.

Quick rule of thumb:Most convenient → Local card shopMiddle ground → Card show / dealerLarge online audience → TCGplayer or eBayPotentially highest net, more responsibility → Private sale

NOT A POKÉMON CARD?

Magic, Lorcana, One Piece & Yu-Gi-Oh!

The basic process is almost identical:

Protect→Identify→Value→Evaluate→Grade if appropriate→Keep or Sell

The biggest differences are which marketplace is most active for that game, how much collectors value professional grading, how rarities are identified, and the size of the graded-card resale market. Pricing tools such as TCGplayer and Collectr can still be useful starting points.
